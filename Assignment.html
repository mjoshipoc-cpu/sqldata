================================================================ 1. THE PROBLEM ================================================================ Every health plan already scores risk from claims and clinical history. But there's a category of member who looks fine on paper — no hospitalizations, no red flags — until you look at where they live. That risk is real, and a claims-only model never sees it ================================================================ 2. THE SOLUTION ================================================================ "We built a platform that combines clinical data with social determinants of health — SDOH — data into one risk score, then automatically identifies which members need help, what kind of help, and drives that outreach end-to-end, from identification all the way to a closed, confirmed intervention. Here's how it works, in plain terms: - We score every member twice — once on clinical and financial history alone, once with neighborhood and social data added — so we can measure exactly how much social factors change each person's real risk. - We identify the population that actually matters — members whose risk is genuinely driven by social circumstances, not just clinical severity. Those are the ones a traditional model would miss entirely. - A 4-agent AI pipeline explains why a member is at risk, what they need first — prioritized, not just listed — how to reach them, and builds a concrete, time-bound action plan. These four agents aren't four independent guesses — they run in a fixed order, and the first two are the foundation the other two build on: - Agent 1 (Risk Analysis) and Agent 2 (SDOH Assessment) are both genuinely AI-generated — each one is a real model call that writes a narrative explanation. They're the "base" agents because what THEY read is not AI output: it's the XGBoost risk score and a separate rules engine that already decided which needs apply and in what priority. So Agent 1 and Agent 2 are each explaining a decision that was already made deterministically — they add the "why," they don't invent the "what." That's also why they can run independently of each other, not because either one is rule-based. - Agent 3 (Channel Selector) and Agent 4 (Campaign Planner) are the "action" agents, and they run after and depend on that foundation — Channel Selector reads the same risk and SDOH profile Agent 2 assessed to decide HOW to reach the member, and Campaign Planner then takes Channel Selector's actual decision as an input to build the concrete plan around it. So the "how" and the "what to do" can't be decided before the "why" and "what's needed" are settled — the pipeline physically can't skip ahead. - Why we built it this way: it mirrors how a human case manager actually thinks — you don't decide how to contact someone or what to offer them before you understand why they're at risk and what they need most. It also means the later agents are always grounded in the earlier ones' reasoning, not making an independent, potentially contradictory call. - Outreach is automated and adaptive — calls and texts go out on schedule, and follow-up messages adapt based on what the member actually said, not a generic script. - And critically, outcomes are human-confirmed. The AI can surface a signal — 'this response might mean the referral was completed' — but a care manager always confirms the real-world fact before it's logged as resolved." ================================================================ 3. THE DATA — WHERE IT COMES FROM ================================================================ "We're working with two data sources, both verified directly from our Snowflake account, both from the Snowflake Marketplace — so nothing here is custom-built or proprietary. First, clinical and claims data, from Synthea — MITRE's widely-used, open-source synthetic patient generator. It produces realistic, but not real, patient histories: encounters, conditions, medications, procedures, claims. It uses real clinical coding standards, and it even includes dedicated modules for social determinants — we've confirmed actual coded findings like 'Unemployed,' 'Homeless,' and 'Stress' sitting in the raw data. Second, neighborhood and SDOH data, from SafeGraph's Open Census Data — built on real U.S. Census Bureau statistics: poverty rate, unemployment, food access, housing, transportation, at the ZIP and tract level. The bottom line to remember: the neighborhood data is real — genuine Census statistics. The clinical and claims data is synthetic but methodologically realistic — necessary because real patient health information can't be used in a demo or proof of concept. Both sources are free and publicly available; we didn't build or license anything custom to get here." ================================================================ 4. HOW THE SCORING WORKS (business framing, no code) ================================================================ "Under the hood, we run two parallel prediction models on the same population — one using only clinical and financial information, and one using that plus the full set of neighborhood and social data. Each model sorts members into four risk tiers: Critical, High, Moderate, Low. Because we run both models side by side, we can directly measure the gap between them for every single member — how much did adding social data change this person's risk picture? We call that gap 'SDOH Lift,' and it's the single number that proves the value of this whole approach: it shows, member by member, exactly how much a claims-only model would have missed. We also don't let the model take a shortcut. There's a composite 'neighborhood disadvantage' score that's tempting to feed the model directly — but we deliberately exclude it from training, because it's built from the same underlying data as what we're trying to predict. Including it would let the model cheat rather than actually learn the pattern. We only give it the raw underlying facts. On top of the score, every member also gets an explanation — the top five factors that pushed their individual risk score up or down. So it's never just a number; it's a number with a reason attached, specific to that person, not a generic statement about the model." ================================================================ 5. TRUST & GUARDRAILS (business framing) ================================================================ "Anywhere this platform uses AI to generate language — for a message, a summary, a recommendation — we built it so the AI can't freelance with facts it wasn't given. A few examples of how, in plain terms: - The AI is handed the facts it's allowed to use, already decided by us — not raw numbers to interpret on its own. If a member isn't flagged as high-cost by our own definition, the AI is told that explicitly and instructed not to describe her that way. We learned this the hard way — just telling the AI 'don't exaggerate' wasn't enough; handing it the predetermined fact was what actually worked. - Wherever a decision doesn't need language generation — building the action plan template, writing the care manager summary, translating a clinical code — we use fixed logic, not AI. No model, no hallucination risk, by design. - The system never generates a message at the moment it's about to send. Whatever a care manager reviewed on screen is guaranteed to be exactly what goes out — messages are only regenerated right after a member responds, using their actual words, well before the next send. - When the AI isn't confident about what a member meant, it says so and falls back to a safe default — it doesn't guess and present that guess confidently. - Nothing closes a loop without a human. The system can flag a 'possible delivery signal' from a text reply, but it cannot mark something resolved on its own — a care manager has to type an actual confirmation before that's logged as fact. - There's one single place in the system that decides what a member needs — so the recommendation list on screen and the AI's own reasoning can never contradict each other. - Every derived clinical code in the system carries a record of exactly where it came from, so any decision can always be traced back to its source. - If the AI service is ever slow or unavailable, the platform doesn't break — it simply keeps the last good message in place rather than blocking the workflow. The pattern across all of this: wherever a decision could be made deterministically, we made it deterministic. Wherever AI judgment was genuinely needed, we constrained it with facts and explicit boundaries rather than trusting it to reason freely. And wherever the system genuinely wasn't sure, we built it to say so rather than fake confidence." ================================================================ 6. Q&A — QUESTIONS TO EXPECT (and the honest answer to give) ================================================================ Q: "Are you using Snowflake Cortex or native Snowflake AI? What model?" A: "No — that's configured as an option in the code but not actually active. It's set up to use Claude Sonnet if we ever switch it on, but the live AI running today is on Groq, a hosted model provider, using Llama 3.3. So there are two models in the picture — Sonnet is wired up as a future option, Groq/Llama is what's actually generating every message and recommendation you see in this demo — worth clarifying before this goes further." Q: "Won't the model become obsolete or go stale over time?" A: "The risk model needs periodic retraining as population and claims patterns shift — that's true of any machine learning model, not unique to this platform. The language piece runs on a hosted API, so those upgrades happen on the vendor's side, not something we have to maintain ourselves." Q: "Does the model have any bias — racial, geographic, income?" A: "Honestly, we haven't run a formal bias or fairness audit on this proof of concept yet. That's a real gap, and I'd rather flag it now than have it come up later — a production deployment should run disparate-impact testing before go-live, especially given how strongly social data correlates with race and geography historically." Q: "How often is the model retrained?" A: "That's not established yet in what we've built — there's a training procedure in place, but no defined retraining schedule. That's an operational decision for production; this proof of concept demonstrates the architecture, not a live retraining pipeline." Q: "What happens if the AI service goes down or is slow?" A: "It degrades gracefully. If a call to the AI fails, the system keeps the last known message in place rather than crashing or blocking the workflow. Members and care managers aren't left stuck." Q: "What would this cost to run at scale — 100,000-plus members, with AI calls for each one?" A: "That's not something this proof of concept tracks yet. The honest answer is that AI cost per member, times how often we re-run things, is a real production question that needs to be modeled properly — good to flag as a next step rather than dodge." Q: "Is this regulated by the FDA as clinical decision support?" A: "This scores social and financial risk, not a clinical diagnosis or treatment decision — so it's likely outside that scope, but that's a compliance and legal determination, not something I'd want to assert confidently in a room like this. That's a question for legal review before production." Q: "Can a human override any AI decision?" A: "Yes, consistently. Every recommendation goes through an approval workflow — draft, approved, rejected, or edited. Care managers can edit messages directly, confirm delivery manually, and escalate or retry any case. This is genuinely one of the strongest parts of what we've built." Q: "What if two different parts of the AI disagree with each other?" A: "They structurally can't — there's a single source of truth for 'what does this member need,' and both the on-screen checklist and the AI reasoning pull from that same place. It's decided once, not independently by multiple components." Q: "Is any of this real patient data?" A: "No. The clinical and claims data is synthetic, generated by Synthea — not real patients. Only the neighborhood and Census layer is real, public data." Q: "How do you reduce hallucination risk with the AI?" A: "A few concrete things, not just a prompt asking it to be careful: 1. We don't let it interpret raw numbers itself — we compute the interpretation first (e.g. 'is this member high-cost, yes or no?') and hand it that as a fact to use, not a number to judge on its own. We tried the soft version first — just telling it 'don't exaggerate cost' — and it still got it wrong. Giving it the predetermined answer instead is what actually fixed it. 2. We tell it explicitly what NOT to talk about — for example, several agents are told outright not to speculate about income or cost, because that data isn't in their prompt at all. 3. Some agents are never given sensitive data in the first place — the message-writing agent never receives risk score or cost data, so it can't leak or misuse something it was never handed. 4. Wherever a decision doesn't need natural language — building the action-plan template, translating a clinical code — we use fixed logic instead of AI. No model involved there means no hallucination risk, by construction. 5. When the AI is genuinely unsure what a member meant, it's built to say so and fall back to a safe default, rather than confidently guess. 6. Every AI-written message is reviewed by a care manager before it's approved, and nothing closes out as 'resolved' without a human typing an actual confirmation — so even if something slipped through, there's a human checkpoint before it affects a real member. The short version: wherever we could remove the AI's need to judge or guess, we did — real judgment calls are made deterministically upstream, and the AI's job becomes explaining a decision, not making one from scratch." Q: "What's the actual training sample size for the model, and why that number?" A: "That detail lives in the training procedure running inside Snowflake, not in the application layer we've been reviewing — so I don't want to guess a number here. What we do know is the platform scores a cohort of 99,999 members today; I can follow up with the exact training sample size and methodology separately rather than state it inaccurately." ================================================================ 7. GLOSSARY — BUSINESS TERMS TO HAVE READY ================================================================ Quick definitions for terms that come up on screen or in Q&A — say these in plain language, don't read the acronym cold. NBA (Next Best Action) The specific recommended action for a member — e.g. "refer to SNAP enrollment," "outreach for care gap." When you see "closed NBA" on the ROI dashboard, that means a recommended action was actually completed and confirmed, not just suggested. SDOH (Social Determinants of Health) The non-clinical, non-medical factors that affect someone's health — income, housing, food access, employment, transportation, neighborhood conditions. The whole platform exists to bring this into the risk picture alongside clinical data. SDOH Lift The core value-proof metric: how much a member's (or ZIP's, or state's) risk score changes when you add social data on top of clinical data alone. A high lift means "this person/place looks fine clinically but is actually high-risk because of their circumstances" — exactly who a claims-only model would miss. SDOH Reclassified Members who cross into a higher risk tier specifically because of social data — i.e., their clinical-only score would NOT have flagged them, but their full score does. Risk Tier The four-bucket classification every member gets: Critical / High / Moderate / Low. This is the headline label a care manager sees. Composite / Full Risk Score The single blended number (clinical + SDOH combined) used to assign risk tier and prioritize outreach. Base Score The same member's risk score using clinical/financial data only — the "what a traditional model would say" baseline used to calculate lift. Neighborhood Disadvantage Score A composite measure of how disadvantaged a member's area is (poverty, unemployment, food access, etc. combined). Deliberately excluded from model training to avoid leakage — the model isn't allowed to see this shortcut answer. Domain Scores (Economic / Neighborhood / Healthcare / Education / Social) The five categories social risk gets broken into, each scored separately, so you can say specifically what's driving a member's or area's risk — not just "SDOH is high." SDOH Burden Index A combined measure of how many/how severe a member's social burdens are — used to help decide how "high-touch" the outreach approach should be (e.g. triggers a Community Health Worker instead of a text message). HEDIS Gap (Care Gap) A missed preventive-care measure the health plan is required to track — e.g. an overdue breast cancer or colorectal cancer screening. "Open" means unclosed/overdue; the platform can trigger outreach specifically to close these. PHQ-9 A standard 9-question depression screening score. Useful context, but the script deliberately shows it can UNDER-capture strain that other data (financial pressure, stress flags, neighborhood disadvantage) reveals — a single point-in-time screening isn't the whole picture. FPL (Federal Poverty Level) The government's income threshold used to determine eligibility for assistance programs. "46% of FPL" means well below the poverty line — the number used to determine SNAP/safety-net eligibility. Z-code A standard clinical diagnosis code used to document social circumstances (e.g. unemployment, housing instability) in a medical record — this is how SDOH findings get formally attached to a patient's chart, not just tracked internally. Care Manager The human who reviews AI-drafted messages, confirms real-world outcomes, and makes the final call (Closed / Escalate / Retry) on any intervention. Always the final authority in the loop. Community Health Worker (CHW) A high-touch outreach channel — a person, not an automated message — used when a member's barrier is access-related (not just lack of information), which a text or call alone won't solve. Escalate / Retry / Closed The three outcomes a care manager can choose at the end of an intervention: Closed = it worked; Escalate = needs a human to step in beyond this workflow; Retry = the approach itself failed, so the channel and campaign get re-decided from scratch, not just resent. Cohort The full population being scored in a given view — e.g. "99,999 members across 434 ZIPs in 11 states" is the whole demo cohort. Approval Workflow The Draft → Approved / Rejected / Edited cycle every AI-generated recommendation goes through before it's actually used — the human checkpoint before anything reaches a member. ================================================================ 8. CLOSING LINE ================================================================ "The short version: claims-only models find the people who are already sick. This finds the people who are about to be — while there's still time to act. And everywhere the AI is doing the talking, there's a guardrail underneath it and a human confirming anything that actually matters."
