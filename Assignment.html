Your zip code predicts your health outcomes better than your genetic code. At EXL, we wanted to test that claim ourselves — not with a hypothesis, with data. For payers, "social determinants of health" often stays a slide in a strategy deck. So we started small: could we actually see SDOH risk at the census-tract level using data anyone can access? We pulled together four sources in Snowflake: CDC PLACES (chronic disease + health outcome estimates) USDA Food Access Research Atlas (food desert indicators, tract-level) Snowflake's US Open Census Data — Neighborhood Insights (demographic + economic context) A synthetic clinical/claims dataset, to simulate how a payer's real book of business would layer on top The hard part wasn't the health data — it was the geography. CDC PLACES comes in at the ZIP-code level; FARA and Census data are tract-level. That sounds like a footnote until you check the numbers: of the ~39K ZIPs in our crosswalk, two-thirds span more than one census tract. A naive join doesn't fail loudly — it just silently misallocates risk, spreading one ZIP's health signal across tracts it doesn't actually describe. Getting this right meant building a proper ZIP-to-tract crosswalk and auditing every join for orphaned records, instead of trusting a clean row count.
